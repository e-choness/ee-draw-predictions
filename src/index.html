How scoring changed on November 3

A new labour market integration factor, worth up to 150 points (140 with a spouse), takes the higher of two scores: Canadian work (the existing points for years worked, plus up to 70 for 12 months in high-wage occupations) or a qualifying job offer in a high-wage occupation. They are never added together. High-wage means the occupation's national median wage is at least 1.3, 1.5 or 2 times the average; 186 occupations qualify. An LMIA is no longer needed for a job offer to count.

IRCC does not publish the pool's occupations or Canadian work directly. The estimate uses only figures that describe the whole pool or the whole workforce, never people already invited, who are a selected group.

  • Canadian work, —: 159,675 of the 240,675 profiles in IRCC's 2025 pool count were eligible for the Canadian Experience Class, which needs 12 months of skilled Canadian work.
  • High-wage share of that work, —: temporary residents working in Canada in occupations open to Express Entry (2021 Census), weighted by how many people work in each occupation. Counting each occupation code once instead gives about 51%, because managers and professionals make up a third of the codes but fewer of the people; the pool's two largest occupations, food service supervisors and cooks, are not high-wage.
  • Points tiers: — of high-wage workers are in 2×, 1.5× and 1.3× occupations (+70, +40, +20).
  • Job offers, — of profiles without Canadian work: 12.3% of eligible profiles in 2024 claimed a job offer, times the high-wage share. For someone with Canadian work, an offer in the same tier never beats the work points, so offers only lift profiles without Canadian work.
  • Category rounds use their own occupations, weighted the same way: — of the jobs in each category are high-wage.

Shares are applied evenly across scores. Profiles that were above the last Canadian Experience Class cut-off were invited, so above it only recent arrivals are counted as having Canadian work. See Method and sources for the calculator and every source.

How the projection works

The cut-off is the lowest score still invited once the round's invitations run out, counting from the top of the profiles eligible for that round. The dashboard works that out on the pool itself, under the rules in force since November 3, 2026.

  1. The pool. IRCC's latest count of every profile by CRS band. Ten-point bands are spread evenly; the 501–600 band thins out from the 491–500 density instead of spreading its profiles over 100 equal steps.
  2. Who is eligible below the last cut-off. Everyone eligible above the type's last cut-off was invited in that round, so below it the type's share of the whole pool is used: 66% for Canadian Experience Class (profiles with 12+ months of Canadian work), and each category's share of the pool from IRCC's Table 17a.
  3. Who arrived since. Above the last cut-off only profiles that arrived since that round can be eligible. While a type's cut-off holds steady, each round takes out what arrived since the one before, so the arrival rate is the invitations per day over its recent rounds, times the days until the next round. The pause adds weeks of arrivals.
  4. The new points. Each eligible profile is rescored: with Canadian work, +70, +40 or +20 if it is in a 2×, 1.5× or 1.3× high-wage occupation; without Canadian work, 100, 70 or 50 for a high-wage job offer. The new factor takes the higher of the two, so for someone with Canadian work an offer in the same tier never adds anything.
  5. Where the invitations run out. Counting down from the top of the rescored eligible profiles, the cut-off is the score where the count reaches the round's size.

The likely range adds how far the same calculation missed when replayed on the type's recent rounds, and how far the cut-off moves if the high-wage share is 10 points lower or higher.

Rounds after the next. Each round starts from the pool the one before left behind:

  • Invited profiles leave. Every profile from the top down to the cut-off is gone; at the cut-off, as many as the round's size allows.
  • New profiles arrive above the type's last cut-off at its recent rate, carrying the new points at the pool's rate.
  • Profiles below the last cut-off are replaced as rounds reach into them, at the type's turnover: the rate that best replays its old-rules rounds two to four rounds ahead. The replacements carry the new points only as often as the pool does, not as often as the profiles invited did. Twelve months in a high-wage job or a high-wage offer is a higher bar than the old turnover had to clear, so high-wage profiles become scarcer round by round.
  • Dates and sizes. Each type keeps its usual gap and size; rounds that fall on a weekend or in the year-end break move to the next weekday or the first week of January. The range widens with the square root of the rounds ahead.

After each new round. When IRCC publishes a round, the projection corrects itself in three ways:

  • It continues from what IRCC drew. The pool is carried through every round held under the new rules at the sizes IRCC drew, from the type's last old-rules round and IRCC's last old-rules count. Half of the last round's miss is carried into the next projection, a quarter into the one after.
  • It refits the high-wage share. The share starts at the sourced default, or at the share that turns IRCC's last old count into its first count under the new rules once that is published. After each round it is refitted to every round held so far. Each miss is weighed against the type's usual miss. Moving the share 10 points costs as much as a 5-point miss, so one round can't swing it far. A share you set in the scenario stays fixed.
  • It learns the offset for pattern rounds. Provincial Nominee, Senior managers and Physicians rounds add their average miss under the new rules, shrunk toward zero: half of it after one round.

Every round under the new rules is listed on the Forecast page with what the dashboard projected before it, using only the rounds before it.

Provincial Nominee rounds invite nearly every nominee, so their cut-off is just the lowest-scoring nominee: the dashboard uses the median of the last four. Senior managers and Physicians scores fall in IRCC's widest bands, so the pool can't place them; every eligible candidate in those rounds has Canadian work in a high-wage occupation, so their recent median gets the new points added (+70 for physicians).

Limits. No public table splits the pool by occupation and score together, so the shares are applied evenly across scores. The census is from 2021, and IRCC's 2025 pool count shows more food service supervisors and cooks (neither high-wage) than the census suggests, so the high-wage share may lean high. Profiles entering during the pause and job offers added after November 3 are not counted. Once IRCC publishes a count under the new rules, the pool view uses it directly, and the high-wage share starts from the share that turns IRCC's last old count into it. Projections keep starting from the last old count and carry it through the rounds held since, because only an old count can be split into who gained which points. The turnover is an effective rate fitted to old-rules rounds: it also covers profiles that become eligible by changing (another year of work, a new test result), not only new profiles. Rounds further ahead assume each type keeps its usual size and gap. These are statistical estimates, not IRCC guidance: IRCC sets each round's size and type itself.
